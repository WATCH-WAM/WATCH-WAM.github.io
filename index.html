Abstract

World-action models inherit broad visual and physical priors from video pre-training. However, standard within-video next-frame prediction allows them to rely on appearance shared between the context and future, rather than learning interactions that transfer across scenes and embodiments. We introduce WATCH, a cross-video pre-training that learns from task-paired human demonstrations collected across diverse environments. Conditioned on the video and actions of one demonstration, the model jointly predicts another demonstration’s actions and future frames from its current observation. Predicting one demonstration from another encourages the model to learn task-relevant interactions that transfer across scenes and demonstrators.

Our pre-training shows that these capabilities can be learned from readily scalable human–human pairs alone, without requiring matched human–robot demonstrations. Analyses before robot post-training show improved motion alignment across embodiments and better action and video prediction on unseen robots, with representations closer to those learned from robot pairs. After robot post-training, WATCH improves MimicDroid in-context success from 40.9% to 68.3% with Cosmos Policy and from 59.8% to 77.7% with Cosmos3, with the largest gains on the unseen levels. Gains persist under context-free post-training across robot embodiments. WATCH outperforms within-video pre-training and co-training baselines, supporting cross-video prediction as a scalable approach to learning transferable world-action representations.

Overview

Predict One Demonstration from Another

(a) Human videos of the same task are paired across collection sources and scenes. (b) Conditioned on the reference video and actions, the model predicts the target’s actions and future frames from its initial frame. The reference’s appearance does not transfer, so what it can pass on is the interaction. (c) After robot post-training, WATCH substantially improves success rates over the base model in both in-context and context-free settings.

Within-video pre-training

p(at:t+C, It+1:t+C+1 | It, st, ℓ)

Context and target come from the same demonstration, so they share background, viewpoint and demonstrator. Prediction can lean on these demonstration-specific cues instead of the interaction.

Cross-video pre-training (WATCH)

p(aqt:t+C, Iqt+1:t+C+1 | Iqt, sqt, ℓ, Ir, ar)

A reference from a different demonstration of the same task supplies the motion; the query’s current frame sets the scene. The model must transfer the interaction into a new visual and geometric context.

Data

Task-Paired Human Demonstrations

Human demonstrations recorded by different people, with different rigs and in different environments, vary along the same axes on which a robot differs from a human. Pairing demonstrations of the same task across these collection sources supplies cross-embodiment variation without any matched robot data. We build query–reference pairs from different episodes of EgoVerse, with human actions expressed in a shared human–robot task space.

Reference-Conditioned Joint Action–Video Prediction

WATCH builds on the joint video–action recipe of Cosmos Policy. The reference video and its actions, from a different demonstration of the same task, enter as clean conditioning tokens together with the query’s current frame and proprioceptive state; the query’s action chunk and future frames are the noised tokens the model denoises. The same slot layout carries over to robot post-training, with a human demonstration as the reference or with the reference slots removed.

MimicDroid success (%) against the collection budget, in units of the full human corpus. Each panel has its own y range.

  • More paired human video, higher success. Success rises with the share of paired human video used for pre-training. With 25% of it, WATCH already matches within-video pre-training on all of the human data on the unseen in-context levels (L2, L3), and it surpasses that baseline at 50% on L3 and 75% on L2.
  • Human pairs are cheaper to scale than robot pairs. Counting the cost of collecting each kind of video (robot video at 8× the cost of human video), in-context L2 reaches 72.5% with the full human corpus, while the largest robot-pair setting reaches 49.0% at about twice that budget.

Embodiment-Invariant Motion from Human Demonstrations

(a) Retrieval, human

(b) Retrieval, robot

(c) Held-out embodiment regression

Cross-embodiment transfer needs corresponding motions to align and the features to keep how those motions unfold. On 359 held-out EgoVerse windows and 396 RoboMIND windows from three robots, WATCH ranks the matching motion from a different human source (a) or a different robot (b) earlier than the original video model and within-video pre-training. A regressor fit on two embodiments and tested on the third (c) predicts future motion from WATCH’s features at a Spearman correlation of 0.18 on human and 0.24 on robot windows, against 0.06 and 0.01 for the original video model.

Joint Supervision Focuses Attention on Interactions

Action slot

Video slot

All three models use cross-video pairing and differ only in what they receive and predict: actions only, future frames only, or both. Action-only attention peaks near the image boundaries and video-only attention spreads over the interaction region and the surrounding scene. Joint action–video supervision concentrates both the action-slot and the video-slot attention on the acting hand and the object, around the faucet and at the grasp, on MimicDroid robot frames the model never saw during pre-training.

On RoboMIND, robot data none of the models has seen, WATCH leads on every metric: compared with within-video pre-training, end-effector MSE drops from 0.046 to 0.039 and accuracy rises from 55.6% to 68.5%, and its video improves over the original video model where within-video pre-training degrades it. On EgoDex WATCH leads on every action metric and on FVD and LPIPS. On Humanoid Everyday, co-training was trained on that dataset, yet WATCH, which never saw it, matches or exceeds it on every metric but FVD, with half its end-effector error.

Human and Robot Pairs Induce Similar Representations

We compare WATCH trained on about 1K hours of human pairs with the same objective trained on about 1K hours of RoboMIND robot pairs. Only the robot-trained model sees RoboMIND during pre-training, so agreement on RoboMIND windows means the human-trained model matches a model trained on the test robots.

On robot windows the human-trained and the robot-trained models agree: every cluster holds both greens, and a window’s two copies sit at a mean distance of 0.03 to 0.06 after alignment, against 0.12 to 0.31 for the original video model, on every robot and at both layers. Matching each window across models over layers 10–27, human-trained WATCH reaches a mean match@1 of 0.50 on robot windows and 0.71 on human windows, against 0.13 and 0.11 for the original video model. Human demonstrations are a scalable source for learning robot-relevant motion representations.
